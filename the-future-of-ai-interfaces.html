The Future of AI Interfaces Is (More) Human

Talking to computers is getting easier. That does not mean screens, keyboards, and hands are going away. It means we finally get to use all of them.

By Matt Hetzel. Researched with Gemini, pressure-tested with Claude, built with both. About a 6 minute read.
Evolution

Four Ways We've Talked to Machines

Every era of computing has answered one question differently: how does a person tell a machine what they want? Nielsen Norman Group describes three major shifts. I split the middle one in two, because the jump from menus to assistants taught us something worth its own stop.

Stage 1: 1940s–60s

Hand It Over

You wrote out everything in advance, punched it into cards, and handed it to an operator. Hours or days later, you got an answer. One typo meant starting over. The machine set the pace, and you waited.

Stage 2: 1980s–00s

Point and Click

Screens finally showed you what the computer was doing. You steered with commands, menus, and a mouse, and every step was yours. You could see the result and decide what came next. The price was learning the machine's language.

Stage 3: 2010s

Ask and Hope

Voice assistants and chatbots let you use plain words, but only for the short list of things they were scripted to handle. Say it the right way and it worked. Say it slightly differently and you got "Sorry, I didn't catch that."

Stage 4: 2022 to now

Say What You Want

Large language models changed the deal. You describe the outcome and the system works out the steps. It is powerful, and it creates a new problem: you have to put what you want into words, starting from a blank box.

What Comes Next?

State of Play

Voice Got Good. Screens Didn't Go Away.

Voice models can now respond fast enough that you can interrupt them mid-sentence, and they pick up on tone. Phones are teaching their assistants to see what is on your screen. Some tools build a custom interface on the spot instead of answering with a wall of text. And then there is the Humane AI Pin, a screenless wearable that tried to replace the phone and didn't last. Same underlying technology, different bet. The products gaining ground tend to keep a screen in the loop.

This direction is already accelerating. OpenAI's demonstration of canvas and multimodal steering captures this exact thesis: speech sets the direction, but humans still need visual, interactive canvases to refine the work. Watch OpenAI's demo for the same interaction model (opens in new window)→

Context & Modality

Sometimes Voice Is the Right Answer

Hands full, eyes busy, screen out of reach: that is where voice wins. A woodworker mid-cut does not want to wipe sawdust off a keyboard. But watch what happens next. She still glances at a screen to check the plan, and she still adjusts it by touch. Voice started the work. The screen and her hands finished it.

Universal Design

Multimodal Isn't a Feature. It's the Original Accessibility Requirement.

A voice-only interface shuts out people who are Deaf or hard of hearing, and people whose speech a system does not recognize. A screen-only interface shuts out people who are blind or have low vision. Neither group is a niche. Together they make a simple case: every important action should be reachable more than one way.

There is a name for that idea. Curb cuts were added to sidewalks for wheelchair users, and they ended up helping parents with strollers, delivery workers, and travelers with rolling suitcases. Multimodal AI is a chance to build the curb cut in from the start.

In practice that comes down to three things. Spoken prompts also appear as text. Visual results have text equivalents that a screen reader can announce. Nothing important is voice-only or mouse-only.

Methodology & Transparency

How I Built This

This page was a collaboration between me and AI tools. I want to be specific about who did what, because that is the part most people skip.

1 Research

I asked Gemini for deep research on whether voice will replace screens. It produced a long, well-organized report examining interaction history, speech latency, and emerging multimodal form factors.

2 Critique

I gave that report to Claude and asked for honest feedback. It flagged a few things: some claims were one-sided, one latency figure came from a third-party test and not the company, several sources were weak, and the works cited included my own uploaded files. I decided what to keep.

3 Sketch

I drew the page on paper and scanned it. These initial sketches laid out the structural progression from evolution cards to the video case study.

4 Content & Production

Claude drafted initial copy and image prompts from my sketch, which I heavily edited for my voice. During this stage, I recognized that a static form prototype and reading speed metrics distracted from the bigger picture. Instead, I pivoted to generating multimodal video concepts with Google Flow, demonstrating the human-AI conversation directly through video and visual format.

5 Build & Validation

I built the page in Gemini Canvas over several rounds and brought in Claude for a second opinion. Using Gemini and Claude together created an effective system of checks and balances—each tool caught edge cases the other overlooked, from color contrast adherence to structural layout precision.
