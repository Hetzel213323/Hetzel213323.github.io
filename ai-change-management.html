AI Change Management: Beyond the Playbook

Tools don't fail because of technical constraints; they fail because there is no one size fits all when it comes to change.

01 • Systemic Inertia

The Problem

People aren't afraid of AI. They're afraid of what happens to them once a company decides it doesn't need them the way it used to. That fear is rational: most of us learned early that the org chart moves faster than loyalty does. But the harder, quieter problem sits on the other side of the table. Leadership often hasn't imagined what the job becomes after the tool changes it, and without that picture, "adoption" just feels like subtraction to the people living through it.

Real change management isn't one framework applied everywhere. Kurt Lewin taught us that you have to unfreeze old habits before new ones stick. John Kotter showed that transformations collapse without urgency and a coalition to carry it. ADKAR breaks it down to the individual level: awareness, desire, knowledge, ability, reinforcement. William Bridges reminded us that the event and the internal transition are two different things, and most leaders only manage the first one. My job is knowing which lens fits the moment, not forcing every rollout through the same playbook.

02 • Demonstrated Track Record

What I've Actually Done

While working at a large state agency, I led a digital accessibility initiative that asked hundreds of people to change how they built and published content, many of whom had no idea their work was excluding anyone. I built champions instead of mandates, and became the person people trusted to ask "am I doing this right?" without judgment.

While working at a higher-ed institution, I rebuilt a professional development program from the ground up, which meant rebuilding trust in a program people had already written off. Credibility came slowly, by showing up accountable, not by announcing a new vision.

While at a state agency, I helped stand up a new data-sharing initiative across multiple departments, something built on a mandate but delivered by working with the people affected, not around them, addressing real confusion about ownership and purpose along the way.

One artifact you can actually look at: the accessibility guidance document I developed for internal use. The harder work was everything after publishing it: sitting with people while they applied it.

03 • Psychological Translation

What Governance Actually Requires

Frameworks and policy documents don't change behavior, people do, one uncomfortable conversation at a time. This is where most AI governance quietly fails: leadership drafts the policy, publishes the principles, and assumes the work is done. Then employees go find their own tools anyway because nobody translated the policy into what to actually do on a Tuesday. A framework only works once someone has walked it through the Bridges "neutral zone," the confusing middle where old habits are gone and new ones haven't landed yet.

My background in psychology and counseling means I don't read resistance as defiance. I read it as information. Most of it comes from one of three places: fear of looking incompetent, grief over a skill that used to matter, or genuinely not knowing where to start. I've been the career changer more than once myself, so I recognize the moment someone's stuck, and I have the patience to sit in the trenches with them until they're not.

04 • Human Capacity

Closing

Adam Grant's research found something counterintuitive: showing people the real human being their work reaches raises both satisfaction and performance at the same time, a combination most workplace initiatives can't manage to pull off together. That's the same principle behind good AI adoption.

The goal isn't just getting people comfortable with a tool. It's helping an organization see the new roles, the new capacity, and the new value its people can create once the tool is doing the busywork. That takes someone who can read the human side of the room and the systems side of the business at the same time.
